THERE IS NO CAMERA.

Nobody left a webcam on the Moon. There is no CAM 1, no CAM 2, and no website from 1997.

The picture is calculated in your browser, right now, from NASA data: the shape of the ground, where each piece of hardware stands, where the Sun and Earth are in the sky for the date you picked, and which stars are up. Give it a date, and it draws that date. That is why it can rewind 57 years.

The hardware is real, and it is still there. This page is about the machines people left behind at one spot on the Moon, and what they found out.

The story of this place

  • 1967. A robot called Surveyor 3 lands inside a 200 m crater in the Ocean of Storms. It bounces twice, takes 6,315 pictures and digs the soil with a scoop to see whether the ground could hold a crewed lander. Two weeks later the lunar night freezes it. [show me]
  • 19 November 1969. Apollo 12's lunar module Intrepid lands on the crater's rim, about 160 m from Surveyor 3. It is the first pinpoint landing: proof that astronauts could be sent to an exact spot. [show me]
  • Pete Conrad and Alan Bean put up a flag, a dish antenna, and a science station that keeps working after they leave. [flag] [science station]
  • 20 November. They walk down into the crater and visit Surveyor 3. They cut off its camera and scoop and take them home, so scientists can see what 31 months on the Moon did to metal, glass and paint. [show me]
  • They throw out their backpacks. The seismometer feels them land. Then the top half of Intrepid lifts off and the rest stays. [show me]
  • 1969-1977. The science station sends moonquake, magnetic-field and solar-wind data to Earth for almost eight years, powered by a small nuclear generator. On 30 September 1977 it is switched off from Earth. [show me]
  • 2009 onward. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter photographs the site from orbit. The footpaths, the hardware and the flag's shadow are still visible. Those photos are the ground you see on this page. [live]

By the numbers

The picture looks quiet. Behind it, every frame is a small physics calculation. Here is what goes into it (counted from this page's own code and data):

1,048,576measured terrain heights, one every 2 metres, from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LROC stereo model). Another 65,536 heights carry the ground out to the horizon, 12 km away.
0.5 msize of one pixel of the orbital photo that gives the ground its brightness. Its 2010 shadows were calculated away so that the Sun of any date can light it again.
5,080stars, every one visible to the naked eye (Yale Bright Star Catalogue), each with its own position, brightness and colour, turned with the Moon's real rotation.
7,707rocks near the two cameras, placed with the size distribution Surveyor 3 itself measured in this crater in 1967: many pebbles, few boulders (218 bigger than 24 cm).
1.9 billionseconds between Surveyor 3's landing and today. The Sun, Earth, stars and shadows are calculated fresh for whichever one you pick. Nothing is a recorded video.
33objects and events, each switched on or off at its documented time, many to the second (touchdown 06:54:35 UTC).
up to 50 millionterrain-height lookups in every frame, only for the shadows: each ground pixel looks up to 56 steps toward the Sun to find out whether a crater rim is in the way.
16.8 milliondepth samples in the shadow map, so the lander, flag and rocks cast shadows of the right length.
1 arcminuteaccuracy of the Sun and Earth positions (Astronomy Engine). At touchdown the page gives a Sun 5.15° above the horizon; the mission documents say 5.1°.
3Apollo 12 crew photographs give the picture its realism: the lander (from the crew's panorama), Surveyor 3 (AS12-48-7100) and the close-up soil grain (AS12-49-7284) are the astronauts' own pictures, relit for each moment.
31sources you can open and check, listed below.
about 2,800lines of our own code: 1,900 of JavaScript and graphics shaders, 500 of HTML and CSS, 400 of Python to prepare the NASA data. On top of that come the open libraries three.js and Astronomy Engine.
0servers doing the work. Everything above is calculated in your own browser, many times a second.

Try it: tap FF three times (one day per second) and watch the shadows swing round, the stars wheel past and Earth change its phase, all from those numbers. [start at the Apollo 12 landing]

Where is the Sun?

For every frame the page asks where the Sun and Earth are, using the same kind of calculation NASA's planetary ephemerides use (the Astronomy Engine library). It also needs to know exactly how the Moon is turned, because the Moon rotates once a month and wobbles slightly. A day here lasts about two weeks, then a night of two weeks.

Check: at Apollo 12's touchdown the documents say the Sun was 5.1 degrees above the horizon. This page calculates 5.15. [show me]

Second check: the 2010 orbital photo has shading baked in. Fitting that shading against the height model gives the Sun at azimuth 272 degrees when the photo was taken. The calculation gives 273.

Why are the shadows right?

Shadows are cast from the real Sun direction over the real ground shape. For each point of ground the page marches toward the Sun across the height map and checks whether a hill or crater rim is in the way. Near landing time the Sun is so low that the crater fills with shadow.

Try the Shadows layer. [shadows off at touchdown]

Why does the ground look like that?

Moon dust does not scatter light like paint or sand. It throws light back toward the Sun, so ground seen with the Sun behind you looks bright and flat, and ground seen toward the Sun looks dark. Honest note: this camera does not show that effect yet. Its ground is lit like ordinary dull paint (Lambert's law). We wrote the real lunar formula (McEwen's Lunar-Lambert model with an opposition surge), but switching it on made the down-sun ground 3 to 4 times brighter than the rest of the picture was tuned for, and there was no time to retune it before the deadline. The ground's brightness pattern is the real one, from the orbiter photo, with an average albedo of 0.11.

Why is the sky black in daytime?

There is no air to scatter sunlight, so the sky stays black even at noon. You still cannot see stars in daylight: the ground is so bright that the camera's exposure is far too short for them. At night the camera turns its gain up, the stars come out, and the picture gets grainier.

Why does Earth stay in the same place?

The Moon always keeps the same side toward Earth, so from this spot Earth hangs about 60 degrees up in the eastern sky and hardly moves. It goes through phases, opposite to the Moon's: when it is night here, Earth is nearly full and lights the ground with blue-white Earthlight. It also turns: watch the continents go by. [show me]

An eclipse, seen from the Moon

When Earth sees a lunar eclipse, the Moon sees Earth slide in front of the Sun. Nothing is coded for it: the Sun and Earth positions simply line up. Sunlight bent through Earth's atmosphere makes a thin red ring, and the ground turns dark copper. On 24 April 1967 Surveyor 3 photographed exactly this from its crater. The page finds that eclipse at the time the NASA eclipse catalogue gives (greatest eclipse 12:06 UTC). [show me] [the copper ground]

How could the science station run at night?

Solar panels would be useless for two weeks out of four. The station used a SNAP-27 nuclear generator: 3.8 kg of plutonium-238 that stays hot on its own. Its heat halves every 87.7 years, so even today it would still give off about 800 W of heat. Its spec sheet shows that number for whatever date you pick.

How do we know the cameras are in the right place?

CAM 1 and CAM 2 can see each other. Switch on Camera markers: the page draws where the other camera should be, using only the coordinates and the height map. If the tripod is not under its marker, something is wrong. The page also tests the line of sight over the crater rim. CAM 1 to CAM 2: 187 m, bearing 145 degrees one way and 325 the other, and the ground does not block the view.

The 57-year coincidence

This page was built on 14-15 November 2026: 57 years to the day after Apollo 12 launched (14 November 1969, 16:22 UTC, struck by lightning twice on the way up).
